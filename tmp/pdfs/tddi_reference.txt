Abstract
Polypharmacy requires accurate prediction of drug-drug interactions to pre-
vent adverse events, yet existing models often lack reliability and explainability.
We propose T-DDI, a descriptor-based deep learning framework for multi-class

dings, T-DDI uses explicit physicochemical descriptors and an uncertainty-aware

spanning 178 interaction types, T-DDI achieves a Macro F1 of 0.8452 on the

Introduction
Drug-drug interactions (DDIs) present a growing challenge in modern healthcare, 
which is driven by the global rise in complex therapies and aging populations [1, 2]. 
Polypharmacy, defined as the  use of five or more medications, has nearly  doubled over 
the past two decades among U.S. adults aged 65 and older. As of 2020, over 40% of this 
demographic reported polypharmacy [3]. This trend is even more severe in high-risk 
groups; for instance, more than 95% of patients with cardiovascular disease experi-
ence polypharmacy, with the majority facing at least one potentially severe DDI [4]. A 
recent study confirmed this trend, showing that the prevalence of clinically significant 
DDIs among community-dwelling older adults nearly doubled within two years, rising 
from 23.3% to 45.2% in those aged 70 and above [5]. Such simultaneous medication use 
naturally increases the risk of overlapping or interfering pharmacological effects. Clin-
ical data indicate that up to 93.3% of patients receiving complex regimens experience 
DDIs, with approximately 21% exhibiting clinically significant fluctuations in biomark-
ers such as International Normalized Ratio (INR) [6, 7]. Consequently, this increased 
exposure is linked to a rise in adverse drug events (ADEs), lower quality of life, hos-
pitalizations, and withdrawals of drugs from the market[5, 8, 9]. Because the sheer 
number of possible drug pairs exceeds the capacity of manual checking, identifying 
harmful interactions is no longer just a logistical issue but a critical safety requirement, 
demanding advanced computational methods for real-world practice [5, 9].
To address this scale, computational pharmacology has largely focused on deep 
learning models that use high-dimensional structural representations. Graph Neu-
ral Networks (GNNs) and their variants, such as MuGNN and MIRACLE, have 
become the standard, using attention mechanisms and contrastive learning to cap-
ture molecular topology [10, 11]. More recently, transformer-based architectures like 
MolTrans have shown the value of substructure-aware attention for modeling explicit 
chemical interactions [12]. Advances in multi-perspective molecular representation,

including models that integrate 3D spatial visual information with traditional fin-
gerprints and graph features [13], as well as graph-based metapath approaches for 
drug association prediction [14, 15], have further expanded the landscape of compu-
tational drug research. In parallel, large language model (LLM) frameworks are being 
applied to drug repurposing through multisource prompt strategies [16], and novel 
global-local graph perspectives have been proposed for drug-protein interaction pre-
diction [17]. However, a common assumption in structural embedding approaches is 
that increasingly complex representations (e.g., learned representations of Simplified 
Molecular-Input Line-Entry System (SMILES) strings or molecular graphs) automat-
ically lead to better prediction. This assumption remains largely unexamined. In the 
context of tabular physicochemical data, such high-dimensional embeddings can intro-
duce unnecessary noise, potentially masking the clear signals provided by explicit 
numerical descriptors [18].
Furthermore, while state-of-the-art (SOTA) models achieve impressive accuracy on 
benchmarks, they often neglect a key aspect of clinical use: reliability. Existing frame-
works frequently operate as “black boxes” that provide high-confidence predictions 
even for rare or ambiguous cases [19]. This issue is worsened in multi-class DDI classifi-
cation, where the data suffers from severe class imbalance [20]. In such settings, which 
involve hundreds of interaction types rather than simple binary outcomes, standard 
models tend to be overconfident in minority classes, creating risks for clinical decision 
support [21]. The challenge, therefore, is not only to predict interactions accurately 
but also to quantify uncertainty and provide post hoc feature-level explanations for 
individual predictions.
In this work, we challenge the trend toward complexity by presenting Tabu-
lar Reliable Uncertainty DDI prediction (T-DDI), a descriptor-based framework for 
large-scale, multi-class drug interaction classification ( Fig.  1). Our  study  makes two 
main contributions: (1) a systematic evaluation showing that, within the architectures 
and datasets considered here, dense physicochemical descriptors outperform attention-
driven structural embeddings; and (2) an Uncertainty-aware Estimator (UE) that 
stratifies p redictions b y c onfidence to  mi tigate th e ri sks of  se vere cl ass imbalance. 
Validating our framework on a comprehensive dataset encompassing 178 DDI types 
and 868,069 drug pairs, we show that T-DDI achieves better performance than the 
compared algorithms in the multi-class DDI classification task, including the updated 
DDI2018 benchmark [20]. We also pair confidence-stratified pr edictions wi th post 
hoc feature-level explanations using Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations 
(LIME) [22] and optional natural-language summaries generated by Gemini [23] in a 
user-centric interface. This study emphasizes reliability, parsimony, and feature-level 
explanation in pharmacovigilance rather than introducing a fundamentally new model 
primitive.

Results
Introducing a large-scale benchmark DDI2025 dataset
To comprehensively evaluate the framework, we curated a large-scale benchmark 
dataset, hereafter referred to as DDI2025, which extends substantially beyond stan-
dard resources. The DDI2025 dataset comprises 868,069 drug-drug pairs (Fig. 1) 
annotated with 178 interaction types, spanning a wide range of pharmacokinetic 
(PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) mechanisms. Compared with the standard 86-class 
benchmark widely adopted in the field [20], our dataset more than doubles the num-
ber of interaction categories and quadruples the sample size. The distribution is highly 
imbalanced (Fig. 2A, B and Supplementary Table 5), with the most frequent type 
accounting for around 13% of all pairs. In contrast, 121 interaction types appear in 
fewer than 0.1% of samples, posing a severely imbalanced classification challenge. The 
dataset is made publicly available at the Data availability section.
Numerical physicochemical descriptors drive predictive
superiority over learned embeddings
We first evaluated the hypothesis that explicit numerical descriptors could outperform 
complex learned embeddings in tabular architectures. As shown in Table 1, T-DDI with 
numerical-only features achieved a top-1 Accuracy of 0.9374 ± 0.0004, outperforming 
all traditional machine learning baselines and deep tabular models including BiSHop 
(0.9275) and FT-Transformer (0.9184).
Crucially, our ablation study (Table 2, “Categorical-branch ablation (DDI2025 
test set)”) reveals a counter-intuitive finding r egarding f eature r epresentation. The 
standard TabTransformer configuration, which incorporates  the  CLS  token, SMILES 
embeddings, and categorical embeddings, achieved an accuracy of only 0.8758 and 
a Macro F1 of 0.7022 on the DDI2025 test set. By stripping away these com-
plex, high-dimensional categorical inputs and relying solely on engineered numerical 
physicochemical descriptors (e.g., MR VSA, EState VSA, SlogP VSA, LabuteASA, 
MTPSA, PEOE VSA, VSA EState; all are explained in the Methods section), T-DDI 
gained +0.0676 in Accuracy and a substantial increase of +0.1430 in Macro F1.
This performance gap suggests that for structured tabular DDI data, dense 
numerical signals provide a more robust representation than sparse, attention-driven 
categorical embeddings, which may introduce noise into the transformer attention 
mechanism. We note that this finding i s s pecific to  th e Ta bTransformer architec-
ture evaluated on our DDI datasets; whether it generalizes to all architectures or 
molecular property prediction tasks remains an open question that warrants further 
investigation.

types (reduced from 86 in the original work), partitioned into training, validation, and 
test splits following the original 6:2:2 ratio [20].

Analyses of mispredicted DDI pairs
To facilitate a mechanistic interpretation of the model’s performance, we aggregated 
178 specific D DI t ypes i nto 3 0 b roader s emantic g roups b ased o n s hared P K, PD, 
and biophysiological attributes (Supplementary Table 4). This categorization reveals a 
highly imbalanced distribution: while groups like “Cardiovascular hemodynamic” are 
abundant, specialized endpoints such as “Ototoxicity” are sparse. Analyzing the error 
landscape (N = 9, 830 mispredicted instances, Supplementary Table 6) reveals that 
misclassifications are not random; rather, they cluster heavily within specific domains 
(Fig. 5). Notably, 87.29% of errors involved predicting a label from a completely dif-
ferent semantic group, whereas only 12.71% were “near-misses” within the correct 
group.
The most prominent error pattern is the bidirectional confusion between 
“Metabolism” and “Serum concentration.” This highlights a challenge in distinguish-
ing the biological mechanism from its outcome: a change in metabolism (e.g., enzyme 
inhibition) is the physiological cause that leads to altered serum levels. The model fre-
quently interprets evidence of metabolic change as a serum-level shift and vice versa. 
This suggests that while the framework correctly captures the broad pharmacokinetic 
signal, it struggles to disentangle the enzymatic process from its systemic result due 
to the high semantic correlation between these concepts in clinical data [32, 33].
A secondary but clinically significant p attern i nvolves “ cross-domain leakage,” 
where PD events (e.g., cardiovascular or CNS depression) are misclassified a s PK 
events. Since PK interactions often serve as precursors to PD toxicity, the model 
appears to overweight upstream PK features, effectively r einterpreting downstream 
clinical consequences as pharmacokinetic disruptions [34]. Conversely, rare semantic 
categories exhibited low absolute error counts, likely reflecting t heir s parsity rather 
than superior performance. The remaining intra-group errors represent less severe 
discrepancies, where the model correctly identified t he b road b iological d omain but 
missed the fine-grained distinction [35, 36].

Discussion
Our investigation into feature representation challenges the prevailing assumption that 
complex learned embeddings are essential for tabular deep learning in biomedicine. 
We found that the exclusive use of numerical physicochemical descriptors significantly 
outperforms architectures relying on categorical embeddings. While the standard Tab-
Transformer utilizes categorical tokens to learn feature interactions, our experiments 
demonstrate that the numerical-only configuration yields substantially higher accuracy 
(0.9434) compared to the variant utilizing SMILES and categorical inputs (0.8758)
(Table 2). This suggests that for physicochemical data, the continuous numerical 
space preserves critical relational information that is often lost when data is quan-
tized into categorical bins [18]. These results support the “data-centric AI” view that 
high-quality engineered descriptors often provide a cleaner learning signal than raw 
unstructured embeddings, unless massive pre-training is applied [46].
Recent studies have further demonstrated the value of richer molecular represen-
tations for biomedical prediction tasks. Zhang et al. [13] proposed a multi-perspective 
framework integrating 3D spatial visual information with molecular fingerprints and 
graph features, showing that fusing complementary structural views substantially 
improves drug discovery performance. Along similar lines, Wu et al. [14] applied 
multisource information and metapath enhancement matrices within a graph-based 
architecture to predict miRNA–drug associations, leveraging heterogeneous biologi-
cal networks to capture non-obvious relational signals. Zhu et al. [15] extended this 
graph-based paradigm to drug repositioning by operating in the frequency domain 
across multiple graph sources, demonstrating that spectral decomposition can expose 
cross-source complementarity that spatial-domain aggregation may overlook. Taken 
together, these works establish that multi-source and multi-perspective representa-
tions substantially advance graph-based biomedical prediction, particularly when the 
task involves sparse labels or heterogeneous input modalities. Our results do not argue 
against these representation-learning approaches; rather, they highlight a complemen-
tary regime: when the prediction task is well-scoped (multi-class DDI classification), 
the training data is large-scale and structurally homogeneous, and interpretability 
is a clinical requirement, carefully curated physicochemical descriptors paired with 
uncertainty-aware evaluation can match or exceed the performance of more com-
plex architectures while preserving a direct link between input features and predicted 
interactions.
Furthermore, the descriptor-based approach demonstrates favorable computational 
characteristics once numerical features are derived. In our DDI2018 benchmarks (Sup-
plementary Table 2; NVIDIA RTX 4090, 24 GB VRAM), T-DDI achieved an average 
inference time of 0.0151 ms per drug pair, compared with 0.25 ms for TabTrans-
former [47] (with SMILES and categorical embeddings), 0.27 ms for SSI-DDI, 3.231 
ms for DeepDDI, and 6.35 ms for DDI-GCN. Regarding training time per epoch, 
T-DDI required 71.01 s/epoch. While this is higher than the SSI-DDI framework

Benchmarking against SOTA general-purpose architectures further underscores

high accuracy with uncertainty calibration, our framework maintains backward com-
patibility with foundational benchmarks like DeepDDI [20] while scaling to modern,

However, in clinical decision support, predictive accuracy alone is insufficient; clin-
icians also need to know how much confidence to place  in a prediction. It is helpful  to 
distinguish between two complementary questions: prediction reliability (whether to 
trust a given prediction) and feature-level explanation (why a prediction was made). 
These are addressed by separate components in T-DDI. The UE provides reliabil-
ity by quantifying model confidence a nd fl agging un certain ca ses fo r ex pert review. 
LIME, in contrast, provides feature-level explanations by identifying which molecular 
descriptors contribute most to each prediction. A confident p rediction i s n ot nec-
essarily an explained one, and vice versa. The integration of the UE addresses the 
critical issue of “alert fatigue” caused by false positives. By selectively deferring low-
confidence predictions, the system achieves higher precision on the remaining subset. 
The threshold-selection procedure, sweeping from 0.50 to 0.99 in steps of 0.01 on OOF 
validation data and choosing the smallest cutoff meeting a pre-specified 0.95 accuracy 
target, was designed to operationalize this trade-off: t he a ccuracy t arget e nsures the 
high-confidence tier demonstrates a clear reliability gain over the unconstrained model, 
while the smallest-threshold rule maximizes the proportion of predictions that reach 
this tier rather than being deferred for expert review. This aligns with the broader 
emphasis in healthcare AI on identifying when a model is unsure, rather than treating 
every prediction as equally actionable [21].
We acknowledge that the primary contribution of T-DDI is the integration of estab-
lished components, including Quantitative Structure-Activity Relationship (QSAR) 
descriptors from RDKit/PyBioMed, the TabTransformer-inspired architecture, LIME 
for post hoc explanation, and standard calibration techniques, into a unified frame-
work optimized for large-scale multi-class DDI prediction. The methodological novelty 
lies not in any single component but in the systematic evidence that this combination 
outperforms the compared baselines, including graph-based models (DDI-GCN [24], 
SSI-DDI [25]) and general-purpose deep tabular architectures, on both our DDI2025 
benchmark and the updated version of the previously established DDI2018 dataset.

case-study assessment. This likely reflects the dominance of enzyme inhibition events

remains an important direction for future work.
Notwithstanding these limitations, this study presents T-DDI, a framework 
designed to make DDI predictions more reliable. By integrating an uncertainty esti-
mator, we provide a calibrated reliability signal that can defer low-confidence results 
and help reduce alert fatigue, ensuring that clinicians receive warnings only when the 
model is most confident.

Methods
Data preparation
This study is a computational secondary analysis of publicly available data from the 
DrugBank database [54]; it does not involve any new human participants, human 
tissue, or animal experiments, and therefore did not require ethical review or informed 
consent. The dataset used in this study was extracted from DrugBank v5.1.12 (released 
2024-04-13) [36], comprising 17,423 unique compounds, with 4,533 drugs annotated 
as having at least one recorded DDI based on the Drug-Interactions-Description field. 
Relevant fields l ike D rug-ID,  N ame,  C ategories,  D rug-Interactions-ID,  Drug-
Interactions-Name, and Drug-Interactions-Description were systematically extracted 
using Power Query Editor (Microsoft Excel) and stored for downstream analysis. Using 
Python (pandas), drug names in the columns “Name” and “Drug-Interactions-Name” 
referenced in the interaction descriptions were mapped to standardized placehold-
ers “drug a” and “drug b”. This procedure was utilized for all recorded interactions. 
Because certain interaction descriptions are semantically analogous but order-specific, 
such as “The excretion of drug a can be decreased when combined with drug b” and its 
reversed form, descriptions were harmonized such that each DDI type was consistently 
encoded regardless of drug order. For normalization, the description encountered first 
during the iteration was designated as the canonical phrase. Subsequent text matching 
and drug name inversion were applied where necessary to ensure consistency, including 
swapping the “Name” and “Drug-Interactions-Name” fields as appropriate.  Following 
normalization, the dataset contains 3,888,299 DDIs spanning 218 distinct DDI types. 
Next, we exclude 18 DDI types annotated with multiple adverse events within a 
single phrase (e.g., “The risk or severity of hypotension, sedation, death, somnolence

Approach overview
Polypharmacy and combination therapy are now routine in many clinical settings, 
especially for chronic and multifactorial diseases [1, 2]. As the number of approved 
drugs and candidate compounds continues to expand, the interaction space grows 
exponentially. Exhaustively testing all drug-drug pairs in clinical trials is neither feasi-
ble nor economical, leaving many combinations clinically uncharacterized and posing 
safety concerns for patients receiving multiple medications. Computational models 
capable of anticipating possible interactions before clinical exposure are therefore 
essential for both risk mitigation and drug development.
To address this gap, we develop a predictive model for DDI classification t hat is 
designed for scale and for practical use. The model serves three roles: expand coverage

ARTICLE IN PRESS
by screening pairs that lack clinical evidence, generate testable hypotheses to prioritize
experimental follow-up (in vitro, in vivo, or real-world data analyses), and support
clinical decision-making at the level of risk alerts rather than definitive therapeutic
guidance.
We represent each drug using 3,780 QSAR descriptors computed from canoni-
cal SMILES (via PyBioMed and RDKit). These features summarize physicochemical,
steric/topological, and structural properties that link chemical structure to biological
effect, allowing the model to learn structure-function relationships without relying on
drug-specific labels or prior interaction annotations.
We develop T-DDI, a deep neural architecture built for high-dimensional tabular
molecular data. Inspired by TabTransformer [47], the model employs a numerical
tabular pipeline specialized for continuous physicochemical features with:
• Input conditioning: Layer normalization over the full continuous descriptor vector
• Prediction head: Multi-layer perceptron (MLP) components applied directly to the
normalized numerical features
• Descriptor fusion: Unified prediction from the concatenated descriptor families
This design leverages layer normalization and MLP-based feature integration while
maintaining strong generalization in high-dimensional descriptor spaces. The archi-
tecture is specifically t ailored t o t he Q SAR s etting, w here t housands o f correlated 
continuous variables capture subtle molecular dependencies that are often non-linear 
and hierarchical. Empirically, this configuration d emonstrates s uperior convergence 
stability and predictive performance compared to conventional deep tabular models 
(see Table 1).
To ensure that model predictions are not only accurate but also well-calibrated and 
reliable, we incorporate an uncertainty-aware ensemble loss that quantifies both epis-
temic and aleatoric components of uncertainty. Each model streams in the ensemble 
produces a probability distribution over all DDI classes; the collection of these dis-
tributions is then aggregated to compute complementary uncertainty measures (e.g., 
entropy, variance, and mutual information), which reflects, respectively, the concentra-
tion of the ensemble’s mean prediction, the disagreement among ensemble members, 
and the information gained from that disagreement. At inference, the aggregated 
uncertainties are normalized to produce a single confidence s core b ounded i n [ 0, 1], 
which serves as a calibrated indicator of prediction reliability. This uncertainty cal-
ibration enables the system to recognize out-of-distribution drug pairs, to prioritize 
uncertain cases for experimental follow-up, and to deliver quantitative confidence 
estimates suitable for integration into decision-support pipelines.
Our approach integrates QSAR-based molecular encoding, a numerical tabu-
lar architecture, and uncertainty-aware ensembling to predict DDI types at scale 
with both predictive performance and calibrated reliability. This combination allows 
the model not only to capture complex structure-function relationships between 
drugs but also to convey meaningful confidence e stimates, f acilitating s afer u se in 
pharmacological research and decision support.

rather than a full TabTransformer with active attention.
The final model was trained as a three-fold stratified ensemble using
StratifiedKFoldwith shuffling enabled and random state 42. Each fold was trained
for up to 200 epochs with early stopping patience of 50 epochs and optimized using
AdamW with learning rate 9.4526×10 −5, weight decay 1.5446 × 10−4, and batch
size 256. We used unweighted focal loss with γ = 1.0 and αt = 1 for all classes. The
active numerical prediction path consisted of layer-normalized continuous descriptors
followed by the MLP prediction head with hidden multipliers (2, 2), ReLU activation,
and cosine-annealing scheduling with Tmax = 50.
The final deployed predictor is a three-model ensemble with 260.055 million train-
able parameters in total, or approximately 86.7 million parameters per fold model
(Supplementary Table 2). Most parameters arise from the numerical MLP head
applied to the 3,780-dimensional descriptor vector with MLP hidden multipliers (2,2),
while the categorical self-attention branch is inactive in the final numerical-only
configuration.

Uncertainty-aware ensemble training
To address the critical need for uncertainty quantification i n c linical applications, 
we develop a comprehensive ensemble-based framework that captures both aleatoric 
and epistemic uncertainty. Our approach trains M independent T-DDI models 
stream using stratified cross-validation to ensure  robust performance estimation while 
maintaining data efficiency.
We employ three-fold stratified cross-validation ( K = 3) on the development data 
only, in which samples are partitioned into stratified folds to preserve the  class distri-
bution across folds. For each fold k, we train a model fk on the union of the remaining 
K −1 folds and use the held-out fold for validation. This procedure yields an ensemble 
of M = K = 3 models trained on different b ut o verlapping s ubsets o f t he develop-
ment data, while the held-out test set is used only for final evaluation. All parameter 
counts reported for T-DDI refer to this full three-model ensemble.
To address the inherent class imbalance in DDI datasets, where certain interaction 
types are significantly u nderrepresented, w e e mploy f ocal l oss  [ 60] a s o ur primary 
training objective:
Lfocal(pt) = −(1 − pt)γ log(pt) (5) 
where pt represents the model’s estimated probability for the true class t, and γ 
controls the focusing parameter. We used the unweighted focal-loss setting in the final 
experiments (equivalent to setting αt = 1 for all classes), so the imbalance-aware effect 
comes from the focal modulating term that down-weights easy examples and focuses 
learning on hard, misclassified cases. This is particularly  useful for rare but clinically 
significant interaction types.

To convert continuous confidence scores into actionable tiers without using held-

validation predictions from the three-fold stratified cross-validation performed on the
development data (n = 694,455; training plus validation). Each development-set sam-

fold, producing leak-free OOF confidence scores for the entire development set.
Candidate high-confidence thresholds were swept from 0.50 to 0.99 in steps of 0.01
(implemented as np.round(np.arange(0.50, 0.991, 0.01), 2)

confidence scale and is fixed a priori as tlow; using it as the sweep floor ensures the
grid covers all operationally meaningful thresholds above the low-confidence boundary.
The upper bound of 0.99 represents a practical ceiling above which coverage becomes
negligibly small. Within this sweep, the high-confidence threshold thigh was set to
the smallest value at which OOF accuracy reached or exceeded 0.95. This target was
pre-specified as a design choice before any evaluation on the held-out test set, which
prevents the threshold from being optimized toward test outcomes. The value of 0.95
was chosen because it meaningfully exceeds the full development-set OOF accuracy,
ensuring that the high-confidence tier shows a demonstrable reliability gain relative to
the unconstrained model. By selecting the smallest qualifying threshold, the rule max-
imizes the fraction of predictions retained in the high-confidence stratum, minimizing
unnecessary deferral of cases the model can classify reliably at that level. We note
that the 0.95 accuracy target reflects an internal operating criterion for this study;
it does not correspond to an externally validated clinical performance standard. The
resulting per-stratum accuracy, weighted F1, Macro F1, and coverage values on both
the development and held-out test sets are reported in Supplementary Table 1, and
the full threshold sweep is visualized in Supplementary Figure 2.

• High confidence: Confidence(x) ≥ thigh - suitable for automated flagging.

and the epistemic uncertainty arising from model disagreement. Together, they 
enable downstream clinical systems to appropriately weight and interpret predictions, 
supporting more explicit handling of uncertainty in drug safety applications.

Rare-class performance analysis
Rare DDI types were defined as interaction  classes accounting for fewer than 0.1% of 
all samples across the training, validation, and held-out test partitions. In the DDI2025 
dataset, this corresponds to classes with at most 868 drug pairs out of 868,069 total 
pairs. Metrics were computed from locked T-DDI predictions on the held-out test 
set. The aggregate rare-class, non-rare-class, and all-class held-out test metrics are 
reported in Supplementary Table 7.
Categorical-branch ablation design
The categorical ablation compared two configurations of the TabTransformer architec-
ture evaluated on the DDI2025 test set. The first configuration followed the original  
TabTransformer design [47], incorporating a classification ( CLS) t oken, SMILES 
embeddings, and categorical embeddings alongside numerical features, so that the 
self-attention mechanism operates over the embedded categorical tokens. The second 
configuration, T-DDI, removes all  categorical inputs and relies exclusively on numer-
ical physicochemical descriptors; in this setting, the transformer branch is entirely 
bypassed and the model reduces to a LayerNorm-normalised MLP over the contin-
uous feature vector. Dropout and attention-specific hyperparameters ( depth, heads, 
attn dropout, ff dropout) are consequently inactive in the T-DDI configuration. 
The two variants were trained under identical conditions (three-fold cross-validation, 
focal loss, early stopping, weight decay) to isolate the effect of the categorical branch.

Fig. 1 Overview of the proposed T-DDI pipeline for drug-drug interaction predic-
tion.The process begins with data collection from the DrugBank benchmark dataset, from which
3,780 quantitative structure-activity relationship descriptors are extracted for each drug pair. These
descriptors are then input into the T-DDI model to learn feature representations; the model out-
puts uncertainty estimates and confidence scores for the candidate DDI types, which are ranked
accordingly. The final output is a DDI type prediction that can be accompanied by a downstream
feature-level explanation.
